Since it is not possible to account for the masses of individual atoms or molecules because these are very small particles, we increase the number of reacting species as written below.

2C + O₂ → 2CO ............ (1)  
2 × 100 atoms  100 Molecules  2 × 100 Molecules  
2 × 10000 atoms  10000 Molecules  2 × 10000 Molecules

Increasing the number of reacting atoms or molecules will not change the ratio in which these are reacting or are being formed.

Increasing the number of reacting species, however, has not solved the problem because this number is still very small. We should increase this number to such a value whereby it is convenient for us to calculate their masses.

Thus

2C + O₂ → 2CO ...... (2)  
2 × 6.022 × 10²³ Atoms  6.022 × 10²³ Molecules  2 × 6.022 × 10²³ Molecules

6.022 × 10²³ is a huge number and we have selected this because

1.00 g = 6.022 × 10²³ amu

Now the amounts of reactants and products in the forementioned equations can be written as follows.

2 × 6.022 × 10²³ × (12.0 amu) = 24.00 g carbon atoms  
6.022 × 10²³ × (32.0 amu) = 32.00 g oxygen molecules  
2 × 6.022 × 10²³ × (28.0 amu) = 2 × 28.00 g carbon monoxide molecules

The mass ratio between the reactants and those of products will then become

C + O₂ → 2CO ............ (3)  
24g  32g  56g

You must have realized that starting from a simple equation we have developed such ratio of masses of the reacting species which can conveniently be used in the laboratory.

According to the above-mentioned equation, 24 g of carbon contains 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ atoms of carbon, 32 g of oxygen contains 6.02 × 10²³ molecules of oxygen and 56 g of carbon monoxide contains 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ of its molecules.

The number 6.022 × 10²³ is called Avogadro's number after the name of an Italian chemist Amaedo Avogadro. Avogadro's number is the number of units in one mole of a substance. This number is represented as Nₐ.
